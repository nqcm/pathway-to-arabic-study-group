    Surah Yusuf — Study Session 9

    Overview

    The main topics covered in this session are:

    • التَّوَابِع (appositives / grammatical followers) — a full recap of البَدَل and its four types, including the بَدَل تَفْصِيل variant
    • عَطْف النَّسَق — the ordinary conjunction we already knew, now given its technical name
    • عَطْف البَيَان (explanatory apposition) — a new concept, deliberately split across two sessions: its two distinguishing rules, why it overlaps so heavily with badal, and why even the grammarians disagree
    • Applying the new rules to the couplet نَحْنُ قَتَلْنَا الأُسْدَ أُسْدَ خَفِيَّةٍ left over from Session 8
    • Āyah 7 — آيَة and its desert-navigation etymology; the verb سَأَلَ in its three constructions; the two forms سَأَلَ / سَالَ and the imperatives اِسْأَلْ / سَلْ
    • لَقَدْ — the omitted oath, and the rule that a جَوَاب القَسَم which is an affirmative فِعل مَاضٍ مُتَصَرِّف must be emphasised with اللَّام + قَدْ
    • اِسْم كَانَ vs. فَاعِل — a correction the class had to be caught out on — and the full rule for when a verb must, may, or need not be made feminine
    • Āyah 8 begins — لَام الاِبتِدَاء mistaken for a preposition, أَبِينَا as one of the five special nouns, عُصْبَة, ضَلَال, and what the brothers actually meant by calling their father "in clear error"

    1. Recap — التَّوَابِع and البَدَل

    Before introducing the new concept, the teacher deliberately refreshed البَدَل, because the two are so easily confused.

    1.1 What Are التَّوَابِع?

    البَدَل belongs to a family of constructions called التَّوَابِع — in English, appositives or grammatical followers. A تَابِع follows its مَتبُوع in iʿrāb, and depending on the type, in other things too.

    The four تَوَابِع
    البَدَل — the substitute
    النَّعت / الصِّفَة — the adjective
    التَّوكِيد — the emphasiser
    المَعطُوف — the conjoined element

    How Closely Do They Follow?

    The نَعت follows its manʿūt in everything — number, gender, definiteness, and iʿrāb. The others follow in iʿrāb but not necessarily in the rest. So "a tābiʿ follows in everything" is a useful first approximation, not a precise rule.

    The Basic Illustration

    قَرَأْتُ هَذَا الكِتَابَ — I read this book.

    • قَرَأْتُ — the verb; تُ is the فَاعِل
    • هَذَا — مَفْعُول بِه, and since it is مَبْنِي, it is مَنصُوب مَحَلًّا
    • الكِتَابَ — بَدَل, manṣūb, following هَذَا

    A second example: جَاءَ أَحْمَدُ وَعُمَرُ — عُمَرُ is the مَعطُوف, joined by the وَاو, and it follows أَحْمَد (the مَعطُوف عَلَيه) in being marfūʿ.

    1.2 The Four Types of بَدَل

    1. بَدَل كُلّ مِن كُلّ (also بَدَل مُطَابِق)

    The substitute is identical in meaning to what it replaces.

    جَاءَ الأُسْتَاذُ أَحْمَدُ — "The teacher, Ahmad, came."

    جَاءَ the verb, الأُسْتَاذُ the فَاعِل, أَحْمَدُ the بَدَل — telling you the teacher's name. Both words refer to the same person.

    Two Common Patterns

    • A proper noun following a title or profession is usually this type.
    • A definite noun following a demonstrative pronoun is likewise this type (as in هَذَا الكِتَابَ above) — the easiest kind to spot, since both words point at the same thing.

    2. بَدَل بَعْض مِن كُلّ

    The substitute is a real, physical part of what it replaces.

    قَرَأْتُ الكِتَابَ ثُلُثَهُ — "I read the book, a third of it."

    ثُلُثَهُ refers back to the book but is not the same as the book — it is a portion of it. بَعْض is a part; كُلّ is the whole.

    It Usually Carries a Linking Pronoun

    This type normally carries a pronoun tying it back to the مُبْدَل مِنْه — the هُ in ثُلُثَهُ.

    The Variant — بَدَل تَفْصِيل

    Listing the parts after mentioning a total number — and here no linking pronoun is required:

    الدَّهْرُ يَوْمَانِ: يَوْمٌ لَكَ وَيَوْمٌ عَلَيْكَ

    "Time is two days: a day for you and a day against you."

    A proverb. يَوْمَانِ is the whole from which the badal comes; each يَوْم is a part of those two days — hence still a بَدَل بَعْض مِن كُلّ, in its تَفْصِيل form.

    3. بَدَل اِشْتِمَال

    Similar to the previous type, except the substitute is not a physical part — it is an abstract quality, trait, or associated attribute.

    أَعْجَبَنِي الشَّاعِرُ إِنْشَادُهُ — "The poet impressed me — his recitation."

    أَعْجَبَنِي الكِتَابُ أُسْلُوبُهُ — "The book pleased me — its style."

    إِنْشَاد is connected to the poet, and أُسْلُوب to the book, but neither is a physical piece of the thing.

    4. بَدَل الغَلَط (بَدَل الإِضْرَاب)

    Correcting a slip of the tongue: you said one word by mistake, then supply the one you meant.

    2. عَطْف النَّسَق — New Name, Familiar Thing

    عَطْف means conjunction — joining two things. The ordinary conjunction we have used all along, formed with وَاو and the other حُرُوف العَطْف, has a technical name: عَطْف النَّسَق.

    جَاءَ أَحْمَدُ وَعُمَرُ

    أَحْمَدُ is the مَعطُوف عَلَيه; عُمَرُ is the مَعطُوف, and it follows in iʿrāb — which is exactly what makes it one of the تَوَابِع.

    3. عَطْف البَيَان — Explanatory Apposition

    3.1 What It Is

    بَيَان means explanation, so عَطْف البَيَان is the conjunction of explanation. The teacher noted the English name she found for it and liked: explanatory apposition.

    It Looks Almost Identical to البَدَل

    This is the single most important thing to hold in mind. عَطْف البَيَان and certain kinds of بَدَل can look exactly the same. You will find the same construction called بَدَل by one grammarian and عَطْف البَيَان by another — including in analyses of the Quran. There is a great deal of overlap, and the difference is genuinely subtle.

    The Anchor Example

    قَابَلْتُ إِلِيزَابِيث مَلِكَةَ بِرِيطَانِيَا — "I met Elizabeth, the Queen of England."

    If I say only "I met Elizabeth," there are hundreds of thousands of Elizabeths in the world — which one? So I supply مَلِكَةَ بِرِيطَانِيَا. That is عَطْف البَيَان.

    And yet — it looks very much like a badal, and some books will call it one. That is expected.

    3.2 Rule One — The Second Element Must Be Better Known or Clearer

    First Distinguishing Rule

    In عَطْف البَيَان, the second element must be clearer, or better known, than the first.

    أَقْسَمَ أَبُو حَفْصٍ عُمَرُ

    A standard example from the grammar books: "Abū Ḥafṣ — ʿUmar — swore by Allah." عُمَر is a far better-known name for him than his kunyah أَبُو حَفْصٍ, so supplying it explains the first. This is عَطْف البَيَان — and again, calling it badal would not be flatly wrong.

    Better Known vs. Clearer

    The rule has two flavours. أَبُو حَفْصٍ عُمَرُ and إِلِيزَابِيث مَلِكَةُ بِرِيطَانِيَا illustrate better known. Clarity is illustrated by dialect:

    The Word for a Mobile Phone

    جَوَّال is the common word for a mobile phone in the Gulf, and especially in Saudi Arabia; other regions — the Levant, elsewhere — use different terms. A visitor who uses a term unfamiliar to their listener and then supplies جَوَّال is bringing in the more familiar word for the sake of clarity. That substitution is عَطْف البَيَان.

    The 'Robot' Anecdote

    The teacher's illustration from her own life: in South Africa a traffic light is universally called a robot. Visiting Karachi, her son was giving directions to her brother — "take a right from the robot" — and got a blank look, until he corrected himself: "oh — take a right from the traffic lights."

    That is exactly the structure: naming a thing with the term you know, then supplying the term your listener knows. In English or in Arabic, the mechanism is the same.

    The Particle أَيْ

    Arabic has a particle that does this job explicitly: أَيْ — also called أَيْ التَّفْسِيرِيَّة — meaning "that is to say." It is a purpose-built explanatory device of the same family.

    Why This Does Not Fit Any Badal Type

    Run through the four badal types: substitution for the sake of clarity does not really fit any of them. It comes closest to بَدَل كُلّ مِن كُلّ, but it is more specific — the defining reason for the substitution is that the second term is clearer. That specificity is what makes it its own category.

    3.3 Rule Two — Where the Speaker's Concern Lies

    Second Distinguishing Rule

    • In البَدَل, the speaker's concern is the second element.
    • In عَطْف البَيَان, the speaker's concern is the first element.

    قَابَلْتُ أَخَاكَ هَاشِمًا — Concern on the Second

    "I met your brother, Hāshim."

    You may have many brothers — but it is Hāshim I wanted to meet. His being your brother is incidental information I am mentioning in passing; Hāshim himself is who I am after. The concern is the second element → this is بَدَل.

    قَابَلْتُ إِلِيزَابِيث مَلِكَةَ بِرِيطَانِيَا — Concern on the First

    My concern is Elizabeth. I have supplied مَلِكَةَ بِرِيطَانِيَا only to tell you more about her. The concern is the first element → عَطْف البَيَان.

    A Student's Test — Reverse the Order

    A student asked: what if we said قَابَلْتُ مَلِكَةَ بِرِيطَانِيَا إِلِيزَابِيث?

    Answer: then it becomes بَدَل. The emphasis is now on مَلِكَة بِرِيطَانِيَا — the first element — and إِلِيزَابِيث is brought in to specify which queen, out of the several there have been.

    The student sharpened the point herself: with إِلِيزَابِيث first, the name alone could be anyone — so the second element genuinely explains which one. The two elements are not carrying the same quality of information. The teacher's response: "exactly, exactly — 100%."

    3.4 The Overlap Is Real, and Context Decides

    One Sentence, Two Readings

    Take قَابَلْتُ أَخَاكَ هَاشِمًا again and imagine two different scenarios:

    1. I am looking specifically for Hāshim, who also happens to be your brother.
    2. I am looking for one of your brothers, who happens to be Hāshim.

    The sentence is identical. Depending only on which scenario is in play, it is بَدَل or عَطْف البَيَان.

    Why Grammarians Disagree — a Methodological Point

    The teacher returned to a principle she repeats often: grammar was reverse-engineered from the language, not the other way round. The language existed first; the rules were extracted from it afterwards.

    "If you have a mathematical formula and you generate numbers with it, your answer is always the same. But if you are deriving a formula from something that already exists, you are always going to have disagreement about labelling."

    So the disagreement between grammarians over badal vs. ʿaṭf al-bayān — including in the Quran — is not a defect. It is what derived rules look like.

    A Student's Mathematical Analogy

    A student offered: بَدَل is like 5 = 2 + 3 — known information on both sides. عَطْف البَيَان is like x = 5 — where x is unknown and needs more information before you know its value.

    The teacher found the observation interesting and broadly agreed, with one caveat: in mathematics things are set in stone; in language they are not. The examples worked in class are textbook cases. In real usage and literature you will meet plenty that are not black and white.

    3.5 Applying It — نَحْنُ قَتَلْنَا الأُسْدَ أُسْدَ خَفِيَّةٍ

    The couplet left unresolved at the end of Session 8 was brought back:

    وَنَحْنُ قَتَلْنَا الأُسْدَ أُسْدَ خَفِيَّةٍ ⁂ فَمَا شَرِبُوا بَعْدًا عَلَى لَذَّةٍ خَمْرَا

    "And we — we killed the lions, the lions of Khafiyyah; so never after did they drink wine for pleasure."

    خَفِيَّة was a place thick with brush, and the lions that lived there were exceptionally ferocious. The poet is boasting of killing enemy warriors.

    So: بَدَل or عَطْف البَيَان?

    Testing It Against the Two Rules

    The teacher admitted she had initially leaned toward عَطْف البَيَان — but running the two tests reverses the verdict:

    1. Is the second element clearer or better known? No. أُسْدَ خَفِيَّةٍ is not more familiar than الأُسْد; it simply adds a further quality — that they were ferocious lions.
    2. Where is the emphasis? On the second element — the lions of the thicket, the ferocious ones. That is precisely the badal pattern.

    Hence the grammarians classify أُسْدَ خَفِيَّةٍ as بَدَل, not عَطْف البَيَان.

    And It Remains Subjective

    The teacher added at once that this depends on how one reads the poem. "It's completely fine if we disagree with the grammarians, or if grammarians disagree with each other."

    To Be Continued

    Deliberately, only the foundation was laid this session. More on بَيَان is coming next week, and the teacher undertook to send Quranic examples of عَطْف البَيَان to the group in the meantime, so the class can grow comfortable with the distinction before building further.

    4. Āyah 7 — لَّقَدْ كَانَ فِي يُوسُفَ وَإِخْوَتِهِ آيَاتٌ لِّلسَّائِلِينَ

    لَّقَدْ كَانَ فِي يُوسُفَ وَإِخْوَتِهِ آيَاتٌ لِّلسَّائِلِينَ

    "Certainly there were, in Yūsuf and his brothers, signs for those who ask."

    A student translated it first; the teacher noted one thing missing and promised to return to it (see §7 below) — a good illustration of how a translation exercise is used as a diagnostic rather than a test.

    5. آيَة — A Sign That Outlasts the Sand

    آيَات here means signs. The teacher shared an etymology she had recently come across and found beautiful.

    Signs in the Desert

    Arabic characteristically moves from the physical to the abstract — the concrete sense comes first, and the abstract meaning grows out of it.

    In a green landscape a traveller navigates by big trees, rivers, lakes, meadows, mountains. In the desert there is almost none of that — and worse, the sand dunes shift and move. Telling someone "turn left at that rock" is useless if the rock is buried by next month.

    So the things that could actually serve as آيَات were the ones that stand the test of time: mountains, and very large rocks. An آيَة was, precisely, a navigational sign that the harsh elements and the shifting sands could not erase.

    Why This Matters for the Quran

    Allah chose آيَة for the verses of the Quran. On this etymology, that says something: these are signs not affected by the passage of time, signs that do not get buried as circumstances shift. The navigational marker that outlasts the desert is exactly the image.

    6. سَأَلَ — Three Constructions

    سَأَلَ / يَسْأَلُ with the maṣdar سُؤَال.

    Maṣdar or Ism Maṣdar?

    The teacher was open about her uncertainty here: سُؤَال could be a مَصدَر or an اِسم مَصدَر; she was not sure which and did not pretend otherwise.

    6.1 Construction One — Two Objects (Asking For Something)

    سَأَلَ can take two مَفعُول: the person asked, and the thing requested.

    سَأَلْتُ المُدَرِّسَ كِتَابَ نَحوٍ سَهْلًا — "I asked the teacher for an easy grammar book."

    • تُ — fāʿil
    • المُدَرِّسَ — مَفعُول 1 (the person)
    • كِتَابَ نَحوٍ سَهْلًا — مَفعُول 2 (the thing requested)

    A مَصدَر مُؤَوَّل Can Fill the Second Slot

    Anywhere a maṣdar can go, a مَصدَر مُؤَوَّل can go:

    سَأَلْتُ المُدِيرَ أَنْ يَأْذَنَ لِي بِالسَّفَرِ

    "I asked the principal to give me permission to travel."

    أَنْ يَأْذَنَ لِي بِالسَّفَرِ occupies the position of the second mafʿūl. (أَذِنَ = to permit.)

    سُورَة طه

    وَأْمُرْ أَهْلَكَ بِالصَّلَاةِ وَاصْطَبِرْ عَلَيْهَا ۖ لَا نَسْأَلُكَ رِزْقًا ۖ نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُكَ ۗ وَالْعَاقِبَةُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ

    "And enjoin prayer upon your family, and be steadfast in it. We ask you no provision; We provide for you. And the [best] outcome is for righteousness."

    Here كَ is مَفعُول 1 and رِزْقًا is مَفعُول 2.

    6.2 Construction Two — سَأَلَ عَنْ (Asking About Something)

    With the preposition عَنْ, the meaning shifts from wanting something from someone to seeking information about something — to inquire.

    نَسْأَلُ المُدِيرَ عَنِ الاِمْتِحَانِ مَتَى يَكُونُ — "Let us ask the principal about the exam — when it will be."

    What Is مَتَى يَكُونُ Doing Here?

    A neat application of the recap: مَتَى يَكُونُ is a بَدَل اِشْتِمَال from الاِمْتِحَان. We are asking about the timing of the exam — something connected to the exam, but not a physical part of it and not the exam itself. Exactly the definition worked through in §1.2.

    سُورَة المَائِدَة — a Weighty Āyah

    يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَسْأَلُوا عَنْ أَشْيَاءَ إِن تُبْدَ لَكُمْ تَسُؤْكُمْ وَإِن تَسْأَلُوا عَنْهَا حِينَ يُنَزَّلُ الْقُرْآنُ تُبْدَ لَكُمْ عَفَا اللَّهُ عَنْهَا ۗ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

    "O you who believe, do not ask about things which, if disclosed to you, would trouble you. And if you ask about them while the Quran is being revealed, they will be disclosed to you. Allah has pardoned that, and Allah is Forgiving, Forbearing."

    The teacher noted that even before this āyah was revealed, the Ṣaḥābah had the practice of hardly ever asking questions — for exactly this reason.

    The Cow of Banū Isrāʾīl

    The cautionary parallel: a murder among Banū Isrāʾīl, and the people came to Mūsā عليه السلام to find the killer, which led to the command to slaughter a cow — the episode that gives سُورَة البَقَرَة its name.

    Any cow would have done. But they asked what kind of cow, and a condition came; they asked about its colour, and more conditions came. With every question the task became harder. That is precisely what the āyah in al-Māʾidah is warning against: while revelation is ongoing, questions will be answered — and the answers will bind.

    And note the mercy in it: "you would not like it if it were disclosed, and Allah is holding that disclosure back from you to begin with."

    Homework 1

    Analyse al-Māʾidah 101 word by word on the group. The teacher flagged it as an excellent exercise in the concepts covered so far, and asked everyone — including those watching the recording — to participate.

    6.3 Construction Three — سَأَلَ بِ

    The preposition بِ carries the same meaning as عَنْ here.

    سُورَة الفُرقَان، آية ٥٩

    فَاسْأَلْ بِهِ خَبِيرًا — "So ask about Him one who is well-informed."

    سُورَة المَعَارِج

    سَأَلَ سَائِلٌ بِعَذَابٍ وَاقِعٍ

    "A questioner asked about a punishment bound to fall."

    6.4 Two Forms — سَأَلَ and سَالَ, and Their Imperatives

    Both forms occur in the Quran.

    تَسْهِيل الهَمزَة — Easing the Hamzah

    سَالَ arises from سَأَلَ by easing the hamzah into an alif. This is a phonetic change, nothing to do with grammar — but it has real consequences for every word built from the verb afterwards.

    Deriving the two imperatives:

    Starting from Muḍāriʿ Steps Imperative
    سَأَلَ يَسْأَلُ drop the prefix, sukūn on the last letter اِسْأَلْ
    سَالَ يَسَالُ تَسَالُ → drop the prefix, sukūn on the last letter → سَالْ سَلْ

    Why Does the Alif Drop in سَلْ?

    Working it out with the class: giving the last letter a sukūn leaves the alif also carrying a sukūn — two sukūns meeting. The resolution is to drop the alif, giving سَلْ. (See [[phonetic-harmony]].)

    سَلْ in the Quran — سُورَة البَقَرَة

    سَلْ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ كَمْ آتَيْنَاهُم مِّنْ آيَةٍ بَيِّنَةٍ ۗ وَمَن يُبَدِّلْ نِعْمَةَ اللَّهِ مِن بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَتْهُ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

    "Ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We gave them. And whoever changes the favour of Allah after it has come to him — indeed Allah is severe in penalty."

    نِعْمَة here is the understanding that came to them — which they then went and altered.

    سَلْهُمْ — سُورَة القَلَم

    سَلْهُمْ أَيُّهُم بِذَٰلِكَ زَعِيمٌ — "Ask them which of them will guarantee that."

    On زَعِيم: strictly it is not an اِسم فَاعِل (the teacher noted it is a صِفَة مُشَبَّهَة, though she suggested treating it as an ism fāʿil for the moment). And زَعْم covers a spectrum: it can describe someone making a claim while believing himself right — and possibly being wrong — and equally someone who genuinely is right and makes the claim. The word itself does not settle which.

    When وَ or فَ Precedes — the Hamzat al-Waṣl Drops

    وَاسْأَلُوا اللَّهَ مِن فَضْلِهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمًا (al-Nisāʾ)

    وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا مِن قَبْلِكَ إِلَّا رِجَالًا نُّوحِي إِلَيْهِمْ ۚ فَاسْأَلُوا أَهْلَ الذِّكْرِ إِن كُنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ (al-Naḥl)

    The underlying imperative is اِسْأَلُوا; when the وَاو or فَاء comes before it, the hamzat al-waṣl is not pronounced and we read وَاسْأَلُوا / فَاسْأَلُوا. See [[hamzatul-wasl]].

    The second āyah also carries its own content: the messengers sent before were always men, and men to whom waḥy was given — "so ask the people of the reminder if you do not know."

    7. لَقَدْ — The Omitted Oath

    This was what the student's translation had missed.

    Whenever You See لَقَدْ, There Is a قَسَم

    لَقَدْ signals a جَوَاب القَسَم — and if a sentence begins as a jawāb al-qasam, then the oath is implied even when its words are absent. The قَسَم here is مَحذُوف (omitted), not explicit.

    How to reconstruct it: wherever لَقَدْ appears in the Quran in this way, understand before it an oath in which Allah is swearing by Himself — by His might and His majesty.

    What Is Being Sworn To

    Read that way, the āyah says: Allah swears by His might and majesty that in Yūsuf and his brothers there are signs — answers — for those who seek them.

    And that is an enormous statement. As discussed in earlier sessions, Sūrat Yūsuf is a story of emotions — of people who mastered their emotions and people who were swept away by them, people who ruled their desires and people who did not. It is therefore the story of every person on this earth; everyone can take something from it. Allah is swearing by His might and majesty that this sūrah will answer a great many of your questions.

    Reflected in the Translations

    English translations usually render this with a bracketed insertion — "[I swear]", "[by My might and majesty]" — precisely because the oath is implied rather than present in the words.

    8. The Rule Behind لَـ + قَدْ

    Why must قَدْ appear here, and why with a لَام?

    The Rule

    When the جَوَاب القَسَم is a جُملَة فِعلِيَّة whose verb is:

    1. a فِعل مَاضٍ (past tense),
    2. مُتَصَرِّف (fully conjugating — not جَامِد), and
    3. مُثبَت (affirmative — no negation),

    then it is compulsory to emphasise it with اللَّام and قَدْ.

    8.1 مُتَصَرِّف vs. جَامِد

    Why the Second Condition Is Needed

    Grammarians say the فِعل مَاضٍ is always مَبنِي — but that is a statement from the morphological (صَرف) side. From the syntactic (نَحو) side there is a further division: some past-tense verbs are جَامِد (frozen) — they have no muḍāriʿ and no amr. لَيْسَ and عَسَى are the classic examples.

    كَانَ is not like that: كَانَ / يَكُونُ / كُنْ — it runs through the tenses and the imperative. It is مُتَصَرِّف. Had the sentence begun with لَيْسَ or عَسَى, the rule would not apply.

    8.2 Examples

    With an Explicit Oath

    وَاللَّهِ لَقَدْ حَفِظْتُ القُرْآنَ كُلَّهُ — "By Allah, I have certainly memorised the whole Quran."

    Here the قَسَم is explicit: وَ + the name of Allah. The jawāb is a verbal sentence, past tense, affirmative — hence لَـ + قَدْ.

    From Sūrat Yūsuf Itself

    تَاللَّهِ لَقَدْ آثَرَكَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا

    "By Allah, Allah has certainly preferred you over us."

    The instrument of oath here is the تَاء; the jawāb is again a verbal sentence with an affirmative past-tense verb, and the لَام + قَدْ duly appear.

    8.3 Applied to Āyah 7

    Run the checklist on لَقَدْ كَانَ:

    Test Result
    Is the jawāb a جُملَة فِعلِيَّة? Yes
    Does it begin with a فِعل مَاضٍ? Yes — كَانَ
    Is it مُتَصَرِّف? Yes — كَانَ / يَكُونُ
    Is it مُثبَت? Yes — no negation

    → Therefore لَـ + قَدْ are obligatory. And the reasoning runs in both directions:

    Reading vs. Composing

    When composing, you check the conditions and then add لَـ + قَدْ. When reading, you see لَقَدْ and immediately know an omitted oath stands before it.

    9. اِسْم كَانَ, Not فَاعِل — and the Iʿrāb of Āyah 7

    Analysing كَانَ فِي يُوسُفَ وَإِخْوَتِهِ آيَاتٌ, the class first identified آيَاتٌ as the فَاعِل — and the teacher let it stand for a moment before catching it.

    كَانَ Has No فَاعِل

    كَانَ is one of the نَوَاسِخ. It does not take a فَاعِل and a مَفعُول — it takes an اِسْم كَانَ (marfūʿ) and a خَبَر كَانَ (manṣūb). The teacher pointed out that nobody had called her out on it: "how come nobody called me out that kāna cannot have a fāʿil?" See [[kana-sisters]].

    The analysis:

    Element Role
    كَانَ The nāsikh
    آيَاتٌ اِسْم كَانَ, marfūʿ — delayed
    فِي يُوسُفَ وَإِخْوَتِهِ جَارّ وَمَجرُور, standing as the خَبَر كَانَ المُقَدَّم (fronted)
    لِلسَّائِلِينَ Left open — see below

    What Is لِلسَّائِلِينَ?

    Two analyses were floated: a second khabar, or a مُتَعَلِّق attaching to آيَات (i.e. functioning as its ṣifah). The teacher chose to leave it, for now, as "khabar one and khabar two" since that is easier to parse — and undertook to check how other grammarians have parsed it and discuss it on the group. See [[mutaaliq-attachment]] and [[khabar-mukadam]].

    يُوسُفَ

    يُوسُفَ follows فِي and is therefore مَجرُور — but shows a فَتْحَة, being مَمنُوع مِن الصَّرف (a non-Arab proper name). See [[mamnu-min-alsarf]].

    10. Feminine Agreement — When Must the Verb Take a تَاء؟

    آيَات is feminine plural. So why is it كَانَ and not كَانَتْ? This opened the full agreement rule.

    The Framing Principle

    The rules for making كَانَ feminine to agree with its اِسْم are exactly the same as the rules for making a فِعل feminine to agree with its فَاعِل. One rule set covers both.

    10.1 Obligatory Feminine (تَاء التَّأنِيث Required)

    # Condition Example
    1 The ism/fāʿil is a real (biological) feminine and is not separated from the verb جَاءَتْ فَاطِمَةُ — جَاءَ فَاطِمَةُ is wrong
    2 The ism/fāʿil is a ضَمِير (pronoun) referring to a feminine — whether real or not see below

    Condition 2 — The Pronoun Case

    Compare two sentences:

    كَانَتْ فَاطِمَةُ ... — here the ism of kāna is the visible noun فَاطِمَة.

    فَاطِمَةُ جَاءَتْ — here the sentence is a جُملَة اِسمِيَّة whose khabar is a جُملَة فِعلِيَّة, and the فَاعِل of جَاءَتْ is a hidden pronoun (هِيَ) referring back to Fāṭimah.

    Because it is a pronoun pointing at a female, the تَاء is compulsory — فَاطِمَةُ جَاءَ is wrong.

    Even a Non-Real Feminine, If It Is a Pronoun

    The striking part of condition 2: it applies even when the referent is only grammatically feminine, not biologically. الشَّمْس (the sun) and النَّافِذَة (the window) are not real females — they are simply treated as feminine in the language. But if the ism/fāʿil is a pronoun referring to them, the تَاء is still required.

    10.2 Optional (Either Masculine or Feminine)

    # Condition Example
    1 Real feminine, but separated from the verb by another word جَاءَ اليَوْمَ فَاطِمَةُ or جَاءَتِ اليَوْمَ فَاطِمَةُ — both correct
    2 Non-real (مَجَازِي) feminine — feminine only in the language طَلَعَ الشَّمْسُ or طَلَعَتِ الشَّمْسُ — both correct

    Remove the Separator and the Choice Disappears

    In the first case, delete the intervening word and the sentence reverts to condition 1 of the obligatory set — the تَاء becomes compulsory again.

    No Separator Needed for the Second

    In the second case, nothing at all stands between the verb and its subject, and the choice is still available. Non-real femininity alone is enough to license either form.

    10.3 Applied to Āyah 7

    Two Reasons كَانَ Stays Masculine

    لَّقَدْ كَانَ فِي يُوسُفَ وَإِخْوَتِهِ آيَاتٌ لِّلسَّائِلِينَ

    1. آيَات is a non-real feminine — signs are not biologically female; they are simply feminine in Arabic.
    2. آيَات is separated from كَانَ by the whole phrase فِي يُوسُفَ وَإِخْوَتِهِ.

    Either condition alone would license the masculine form; here both apply.

    11. Āyah 8 Begins — إِذْ قَالُوا

    إِذْ قَالُوا لَيُوسُفُ وَأَخُوهُ أَحَبُّ إِلَىٰ أَبِينَا مِنَّا وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ إِنَّ أَبَانَا لَفِي ضَلَالٍ مُّبِينٍ

    "When they said: 'Surely Yūsuf and his brother are more beloved to our father than we are — while we are a strong band. Indeed our father is in clear error.'"

    A student translated first, and several corrections followed — corrections the teacher made immediately and deliberately, so that nobody carried a wrong reading away over the two-week break.

    11.1 لَيُوسُفُ — لَام الاِبتِدَاء, Not a Preposition

    The Key Correction

    The student read لَيُوسُفُ as "[they said] to Yūsuf" — taking the لَام as the preposition لِ.

    It is not. It is لَام الاِبتِدَاء — the lām of emphasis. The meaning is "indeed Yūsuf…", and Yūsuf is the مُبتَدَأ of what they said, not the person addressed. The brothers are speaking among themselves, not to him.

    11.2 وَأَخُوهُ — His Brother, Singular

    Not "brothers." أَخُوهُ is singular — his one brother, Binyāmīn, the full brother from the same mother.

    11.3 أَبِينَا — One of the Five Special Nouns

    أَب is one of الأَسمَاء الخَمسَة, which show their case with a letter rather than a ḥarakah:

    Case Form
    Marfūʿ أَبُونَا
    Manṣūb أَبَانَا
    Majrūr أَبِينَا

    After إِلَىٰ it is مَجرُور, hence أَبِينَا. And in إِنَّ أَبَانَا it is the اِسْم إِنَّ, hence مَنصُوب — أَبَانَا. See [[five-special-nouns]].

    11.4 مِنَّا — "Than Us," Not "From Us"

    أَحَبُّ إِلَىٰ أَبِينَا مِنَّا is a comparative: more beloved to our father than we are. The مِن is the مِن of comparison, not the partitive or ablative one.

    12. عُصْبَة

    A Word Only Used of Men

    عُصْبَة denotes a strong band or group — and it is used only for men, specifically men roughly between 10 and 40 years of age: the young, healthy, physically capable.

    After 40 a man is no longer at that peak of physical labour; and although ten years old sounds like a child to us, in those times boys of eleven or twelve were already working.

    Plural: عُصْبَة → عُصَب, on the pattern فُعْلَة → فُعَل — like غُرْفَة → غُرَف and ظُلْمَة → ظُلَم.

    The Social Logic Behind the Brothers' Complaint

    Grown sons were assets: they could farm, they could shepherd, they multiplied the household's material worth. Young children were, in that reckoning, a liability — to be fed and raised before they returned anything.

    So the brothers' grievance has an internal logic: we are ten, grown, strong, and productive; Yūsuf and his brother are two small children. On a purely material accounting, our father should love us more.

    The teacher was careful to add two things: this materialistic mindset has been present in every age — "we are seeing the height of materialism now" — and it emphatically does not describe Yaʿqūb عليه السلام. It describes the brothers' assumptions.

    13. ضَلَال and the لَام المُزَحلَقَة

    13.1 لَفِي — the Slippery Lām

    إِنَّ أَبَانَا لَفِي ضَلَالٍ مُّبِينٍ

    The لَام in لَفِي is the لَام المُزَحلَقَة — the "slippery lām." It belongs at the head of the sentence with إِنَّ, but is pushed forward onto the khabar. See [[lam-muzaalaqa]].

    13.2 The Verb ضَلَّ

    Arabic Note
    ضَلَّ عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ With the preposition عَنْ — he strayed from the way
    ضَلَّ الطَّرِيقَ Directly transitive — both constructions are valid
    ضَلَال / ضَلَالَة Both are maṣdars

    Meaning: to move away from the correct path — and not to return to it, not to be guided back. It is the opposite of هُدَى.

    The Same Word in al-Fātiḥah

    وَلَا الضَّالِّينَ

    الضَّالِّينَ is the plural of الضَّالّ, formed as a جَمْع مُذَكَّر سَالِم.

    Iʿrāb Check — How Do We Know It Is Majrūr?

    A student noticed the فَتْحَة visible on the word and hesitated. The teacher's question: what is the sign of its being majrūr?

    Answer: the يَاء. In a جَمْع مُذَكَّر سَالِم:

    Case Sign
    Marfūʿ وَاو
    Manṣūb يَاء
    Majrūr يَاء

    So الضَّالِّينَ is مَجرُور (as a مَعطُوف following المَغْضُوبِ عَلَيْهِمْ), and its sign is the يَاء — the fatḥah sitting on the nūn is not the case marker at all. See [[jama-muzakar-salim]].

    13.3 What Did the Brothers Actually Mean?

    Not Religious Misguidance

    This is a crucial point of tafsīr. ضَلَال here is not deviation in matters of دِين. Yaʿqūb عليه السلام was a prophet — had the brothers meant that he had gone astray in religion, they would have been making a statement of kufr.

    What they meant was that he was straying from the sensible course: that he was moving away from what logic (as they saw it) dictated — favouring two young children over ten grown, strong sons who were, materially, far more valuable to him.

    In their assessment, "he is not thinking correctly."

    مُبِين completes it: clearly so, in their eyes.

    The Source of This Reading

    Dr. V. Abdur Raheem is here paraphrasing this understanding from an earlier tafsīr — he has taken and reworked the meaning rather than originating it.

    16. Key Lessons from This Session

    Summary of Lessons

    1. التَّوَابِع are a family — بَدَل، نَعت، تَوكِيد، مَعطُوف — united by following their مَتبُوع in iʿrāb. Only the نَعت follows in everything.
    2. عَطْف البَيَان is distinguished from بَدَل by exactly two things: the second element must be clearer or better known, and the speaker's concern lies with the first element rather than the second.
    3. Reversing the order of the two elements can flip the analysis from ʿaṭf al-bayān to badal — a good test to run when unsure.
    4. The overlap between the two is genuine and context-dependent: the identical sentence can be either, depending only on what the speaker was after.
    5. Grammarians disagreeing — even about the Quran — is what reverse-engineered rules look like. Language came first; grammar was extracted from it afterwards. Deriving a formula from existing data will always leave disputed labels.
    6. Running the two ʿaṭf al-bayān tests on أُسْدَ خَفِيَّةٍ reverses a first impression and lands on بَدَل — a demonstration that the tests do real work rather than merely naming what you already assumed.
    7. آيَة originally meant a desert navigational sign robust enough to survive shifting dunes — which gives the Quran's own آيَات a striking resonance: signs unaffected by the passage of time.
    8. سَأَلَ has three distinct constructions — two objects (asking for), عَنْ (asking about), and بِ (same sense as عن) — and reading the preposition tells you which meaning is in play.
    9. تَسْهِيل الهَمزَة is a purely phonetic change, but it propagates into every word derived afterwards — which is how one verb ends up with two valid imperatives, اِسْأَلْ and سَلْ.
    10. لَقَدْ is a marker of an omitted oath. Seeing it, reconstruct Allah swearing by His might and majesty — and read the statement that follows with the weight that implies.
    11. The لَام + قَدْ are obligatory when the jawāb al-qasam is a verbal sentence with an affirmative, مُتَصَرِّف past-tense verb — which is why the distinction between كَانَ (mutaṣarrif) and لَيْسَ / عَسَى (jāmid) matters syntactically, not just morphologically.
    12. كَانَ takes an اِسْم and a خَبَر, never a فَاعِل — an easy slip, and one worth having a class catch.
    13. Feminine agreement is one rule set covering both fiʿl/fāʿil and kāna/ismuhā. It is obligatory for an unseparated real feminine and for any pronoun referring to a feminine; optional for a separated real feminine and for a non-real feminine.
    14. In Āyah 7 both optional-case conditions hold at once — آيَات is a non-real feminine and is separated from كَانَ — which is why the masculine كَانَ stands.
    15. A لَام at the head of a word is not automatically the preposition: لَيُوسُفُ is لَام الاِبتِدَاء, and misreading it changes who is being addressed in the whole āyah.
    16. In a جَمْع مُذَكَّر سَالِم, the case sign is the وَاو or the يَاء — never the ḥarakah visible on the nūn.
    17. ضَلَال in the brothers' speech is not religious misguidance — that reading would make their words kufr against a prophet. They meant he was straying from what they took to be the sensible, material calculus.
    18. On teaching: "I am not a teacher, I am a student. By teaching you I am strengthening my own understanding — there are so many things where I never knew the gap was there until I started explaining it."

    Āyah 7 is complete and Āyah 8 has been opened. The class breaks for two weeks; next session resumes with more on عَطْف البَيَان, then completes Āyah 8, in shāʾ Allāh.
