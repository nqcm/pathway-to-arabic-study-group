    Surah Yusuf — Study Session 14

    Overview

    The main topics covered in this session are:

    • More meanings of the jārr lām — لَام التَّعلِيل with nouns; the lām of the end boundary (كُلٌّ يَجْرِي لِأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى); the lām of oath
    • رَبُّنَا يَعْلَمُ as an oath (Yā-Sīn 16) — why saying Allah knows is so grave
    • The four meanings of جَعَلَ — to make into, to assume, to create, to begin; Firʿawn and شِيَعًا, the angle of Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ
    • لَمَّا — when vs not yet; ẓarf or ḥarf; the forms of its jawāb; إِذَا الفُجَائِيَّة and فَ in the jawāb; بَرّ / بِرّ; كَفُور and the كُفَّار of al-Ḥadīd
    • Where is the jawāb of فَلَمَّا in Āyah 15? — the Baṣrī omitted jawāb vs the Kūfī extra wāw (وَاو الاِقتِحَام); al-Ṣāffāt 103–104; the open gates of al-Zumar 73; al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī
    • لَتُنَبِّئَنَّهُم — the three conditions for lām + nūn in the jawāb of an oath, and what happens when each one fails

    A Note on These Notes

    The source captions drop nearly all Arabic. The Arabic below has been restored from the context of the lesson and from the Quran where an āyah is identifiable, and the book's sentences from the teacher's translations. Where a word or example could not be recovered with confidence, the notes say so instead of guessing.

    1. Recap — What ʿAmal Means

    The class's chart of the ḥurūf moved over from the shared whiteboard to Notability, "because I have the laser pointer", so the teacher can point to the thing being discussed.

    Last time the class saw that some ḥurūf fall into multiple categories, and that the lām is one of them.

    ʿAmal Is Always About the Iʿrāb

    A عَامِلَة particle makes some change happen to its مَعمُول. A non-working particle obviously does have a function, but it changes nothing in the iʿrāb of the thing.

    When we speak of the عَمَل of a ḥarf, "it does not mean the syntactic like meaning or all those kind of things. It means what changes does it affect."

    The working lāms again:

    Kind Effect Note
    الجَازِمَة Gives jazm to the muḍāriʿ
    الجَارَّة Makes its noun majrūr Many meanings, being worked through now
    النَّاصِبَة Found in some modern books Many classical grammarians reject it: it is still a lām of jarr, and a hidden أَنْ after it does the naṣb

    So classically speaking there are only two working kinds. Within الجَارَّة the class has done لَام التَّقوِيَة, then last time المِلك and شِبه المِلك (also called لَام الاِستِحقَاق and لَام الاِختِصَاص).

    Shibh al-Milk Is Everywhere

    Whenever something cannot legally or logically be owned by whoever the lām is on, you have شِبه المِلك. Things for animals are the constant example, because animals cannot legally own anything. (It was left off the chart; think of it as part of number three.)

    See A Map of the Lāms.

    2. More Meanings of the Jārr Lām

    2.1 لَام التَّعلِيل with Nouns

    The class knows this one from the muḍāriʿ, but it is the same lām with verbs and with nouns. تَعلِيل means giving the reason: one thing is the reason or necessity for the other.

    Few examples with nouns have come up so far, so here is one:

    الاِجتِهَادُ لِلنَّجَاحِ ضَرُورِيٌّ — Hard work is necessary for success. (the wording is reconstructed from the teacher's translation)

    A student offered a sentence with hopes (lost in the captions). With a verb, the muḍāriʿ is manṣūb because a hidden أَنْ comes after the lām, and that أَنْ plus the verb makes an implied maṣdar, which the lām makes majrūr. A second example:

    جِئْتُ لِأَلْتَقِيَ بِكَ — I came to meet you. (the teacher corrected a detail of the wording, which the captions lost)

    2.2 The Lām of the Boundary

    The next meaning is the lām of the end of a limit (the name is lost in the captions; other notes call it لَام الغَايَة, and اِنتِهَاء الغَايَة is the usual term). You can think of it as a boundary, and it can be of two kinds: of time (زَمَان) or of place (مَكَان).

    Space-Time

    "If you have studied general theory of relativity, then you will say that [they] are together space-time." Theoretical physics is the teacher's new muse: next on the reading list is The God Equation, and the family Kindle holds The Elegant Universe and Is God a Mathematician? Some students share the interest, at a popular level, along with new theories about consciousness.

    وَسَخَّرَ الشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ ۖ كُلٌّ يَجْرِي لِأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى (al-Raʿd 2)

    "He subjected the sun and the moon, each running for a term appointed."

    Everything is moving, floating, till a time. When the appointed time comes, this movement ceases. So the lām here marks the outer limit of the time, after which the action stops.

    Word Note
    أَجَل A term, a time
    سَمَّى / يُسَمِّي To call something by a name, from اِسْم
    مُسَمًّى Named, hence an appointed time

    Time or Place?

    Some have taken this phrase to mean an appointed place, but the majority hold that it is a time.

    The same idea can also be expressed with another word, meaning until (most likely إِلَى, as in كُلٌّ يَجْرِي إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى, Luqmān 29; the captions lost it).

    2.3 One Ḥarf, Very Different Meanings

    Grammatically One, Meaning-Wise Many

    Lām What it says
    المِلك Belonging
    شِبه المِلك Sort of belonging
    التَّعلِيل A reason
    The boundary None of those: the limit of the time

    All are one ḥarf as far as grammar goes, since they have the same rule and make the noun majrūr. "Once you get beyond the beginners level", you start unpacking the different meanings within the same ḥarf.

    The teacher skipped number five on the list for now, to spend time on it later, and went on to number six.

    2.4 The Lām of Oath

    The class has done the particles of oath. The oath can also be taken with a lām (the teacher's sentence is lost; compare لِلَّهِ لَأَفْعَلَنَّ, by Allah, I will surely do it).

    Not For Allah

    Here لِلَّهِ does not mean for Allah. It means I am taking an oath, the same meaning as وَاللَّهِ.

    This usage is not very common. It is not found in the Quran in this way. Most of the time an oath comes with the usual particles or the word for swearing itself.

    See Lām al-Jār and Ḥurūf al-Qasam.

    2.5 رَبُّنَا يَعْلَمُ — Another Way of Swearing

    The teacher is currently studying Sūrah Yā-Sīn with their children, and the āyah comes from the story of the messengers sent to a town. When the people reject them:

    قَالُوا رَبُّنَا يَعْلَمُ إِنَّا إِلَيْكُمْ لَمُرْسَلُونَ (Yā-Sīn 16)

    "They said, 'Our Lord knows that we have indeed been sent to you.'"

    رَبُّنَا يَعْلَمُ is in the place of an oath. An oath is bringing Allah as a witness to the truthfulness of your statement, and that is exactly what this statement does.

    The Emphasis Gives It Away

    Āyah Text Why
    14 إِنَّا إِلَيْكُم مُّرْسَلُونَ Plain emphasis, so no lām
    16 إِنَّا إِلَيْكُمْ لَمُرْسَلُونَ The jawāb of an oath: a jumlah ismiyyah must take إِنَّ + lām

    A Graver Statement Than an Oath

    According to Ḥanafī jurists, a false oath is a huge sin, but it is not kufr. But if someone says Allah knows, even in English, about something untrue, that is technically kufr.

    Other scholars have rejected this and say it is still only a grave sin (the captions read "grave kufr" here, which does not fit the contrast being drawn). The teacher was not going into that debate, and said we should not start labelling people on this basis. The two reasons for mentioning it:

    1. Saying Allah knows is a kind of oath, so be really careful about using it.
    2. It is, in many circumstances, graver than a normal oath.

    3. The Four Meanings of جَعَلَ

    Back to the book, which goes over the four meanings the class has already done, so the focus here is on the Arabic reading.

    3.1 To Make One Thing into Another

    A few other verbs work like this, and one of them has the same meaning (lost in the captions; most likely صَيَّرَ). It takes two mafʿūls.

    جَعَلْتُ هَذِهِ الغُرْفَةَ مَكْتَبَةً — I made this room into a library.

    جَعَلْنَا هَذَا البَيْتَ مَسْجِدًا — We made this house into a masjid.

    (The wording is reconstructed from the translations.) The second is a very common practice in non-Muslim countries where Muslims moved and started buying houses.

    From the Quran:

    إِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ عَلَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَجَعَلَ أَهْلَهَا شِيَعًا يَسْتَضْعِفُ طَائِفَةً مِّنْهُمْ يُذَبِّحُ أَبْنَاءَهُمْ وَيَسْتَحْيِي نِسَاءَهُمْ ۚ إِنَّهُ كَانَ مِنَ الْمُفْسِدِينَ (al-Qaṣaṣ 4)

    "Verily Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and made its people into sects, oppressing a group of them, slaughtering their sons and letting their females live. Indeed, he was of the corrupters."

    أَهْلَهَا is mafʿūl number one, and شِيَعًا is number two.

    The Flavour of the Story in al-Qaṣaṣ

    Whenever something is repeated in the Quran, it is never the same. The story of Mūsā عليه السلام occurs in many sūrahs, with a different flavour each time, and the start of a sūrah gives an overview of what it will be about.

    A student said this sūrah has his early childhood up until his marriage. But its opening (نَتْلُو عَلَيْكَ مِن نَّبَإِ مُوسَىٰ وَفِرْعَوْنَ, āyah 3) says straight away that this time the story is about Mūsā and Firʿawn: not Mūsā in his personal capacity or his personal growth as a nabī, but the story from the perspective of the resistance, the rebellion against tyranny, and the whole community.

    And it starts with a mother (وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ أُمِّ مُوسَىٰ, āyah 7), "which is telling us that a lot of revolutions do start in the mothers' cradles."

    شِيعَة — A Group

    شِيَع means groups or sects. The people called Shīʿah were at first شِيعَةُ عَلِيّ, the group of ʿAlī: in the early clash (the names are lost in the captions), a group sided with ʿAlī and began calling themselves that, and the name was shortened.

    Divide and rule is a very common strategy for today's politicians as well, who make you fight over race, religion and ethnicity. The teacher pointed to the xenophobia in South Africa: not the normal local people, but politically ignited ones trying to send foreigners away, saying they are taking jobs. "It's an age-old method."

    Word Note
    عَلَا To become high, powerful
    يَسْتَضْعِفُ Root ض-ع-ف, weakness, in Form X, which brings the meaning of seeking: to seek someone's weakness, to oppress
    يَسْتَحْيِي نِسَاءَهُمْ Letting their women live: a delicate way of putting what these women were being put through, to enslave and harass them

    3.2 To Assume

    Like ظَنَّ, this also takes two mafʿūls.

    أَجَعَلْتَنِي مِثْلَكَ؟ — Do you think I am like you? (reconstructed; literally have you taken me to be like you?)

    تَ is the fāʿil; نِي is the nūn of protection and the yāʾ as mafʿūl number one; مِثْلَكَ is number two.

    وَجَعَلُوا الْمَلَائِكَةَ الَّذِينَ هُمْ عِبَادُ الرَّحْمَٰنِ إِنَاثًا ۚ أَشَهِدُوا خَلْقَهُمْ ۚ سَتُكْتَبُ شَهَادَتُهُمْ وَيُسْأَلُونَ (al-Zukhruf 19)

    "And they have taken the angels, who are servants of the Most Merciful, as females. Did they witness their creation? Their testimony will be recorded, and they will be questioned."

    Part Role
    وَ of جَعَلُوا Fāʿil
    الْمَلَائِكَةَ Mafʿūl number one
    الَّذِينَ Naʿt of الْمَلَائِكَةَ, with هُمْ عِبَادُ الرَّحْمَٰنِ as its ṣilah
    إِنَاثًا Mafʿūl number two

    The middle part is further information: move it out, and what remains is a complete sentence. Tafsīrs have a lot of discussion on why Allah chose to place it there.

    Our Words Are Written

    "The words we utter without thinking, they are grave." Any words we say are being written down, and we will be questioned about them.

    3.3 To Create

    Here it means خَلَقَ, and it takes only one mafʿūl.

    الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَجَعَلَ الظُّلُمَاتِ وَالنُّورَ ۖ ثُمَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِرَبِّهِمْ يَعْدِلُونَ (al-Anʿām 1)

    "All praise is for Allah, who created the heavens and the earth and made the darknesses and the light. Yet those who disbelieve hold others as equal with their Lord."

    Many Darknesses, One Light

    The ways which go away from Allah are many, but the light is always one, because that is the light of Allah.

    Is النُّورَ Mafʿūl Number Two?

    No. It is مَعطُوف on الظُّلُمَاتِ. The sign of الظُّلُمَاتِ being manṣūb is a kasrah, since it is a sound feminine plural; that does not mean it is majrūr. النُّورَ is manṣūb because it follows it, with a fatḥah because it is an ordinary word.

    يَعْدِلُونَ is making something equal. Justice is about putting things in their correct places; they are putting someone in the same place as Allah.

    3.4 To Begin

    Now it is one of the sisters, and it takes an ism and a khabar rather than a mafʿūl (one of the verbs of الشُّرُوع, from شَرَعَ, to start).

    جَعَلَ الأُسْتَاذُ يَشْرَحُ الدَّرْسَ — The teacher started explaining the lesson. (reconstructed)

    The String Attached

    In the meaning of starting, its khabar must always be a sentence with a muḍāriʿ. You cannot have a single word as its khabar.

    See جَعَلَ — Four Meanings and Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ.

    4. لَمَّا in Āyah 15

    The book now returns to:

    فَلَمَّا ذَهَبُوا بِهِ وَأَجْمَعُوا أَن يَجْعَلُوهُ فِي غَيَابَتِ الْجُبِّ ۚ وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْهِ لَتُنَبِّئَنَّهُم بِأَمْرِهِمْ هَٰذَا وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

    The class had been discussing جَعَلَ in this āyah and now went back to its beginning, فَلَمَّا.

    4.1 Two Kinds of لَمَّا

    لَمَّا Meaning Note
    The one in this āyah When Gives a moment in time when an action takes place
    الجَازِمَة Not yet Has your father come? لَمَّا — not yet. A different one

    The one in this āyah is an adverb of time, and it is specifically for the past: its meaning is always frozen in the past, and its jawāb is too. So it takes a māḍī, and has a jawāb that is also māḍī. We know the meaning is past when we come to translate it:

    When I heard the […], I went to the […]. (compare the same book's لَمَّا سَمِعْتُ الأَذَانَ ذَهَبْتُ إِلَى الْمَسْجِدِ; the captions are fragmentary)

    4.2 Particle or Adverb?

    The book's footnote takes up what لَمَّا is. The main text treats it as an adverb, which is an ism in Arabic and not a particle. But some grammarians (the name is lost) call it a particle, with a particular name (the book's verb is once again سَمَّى; the name is lost, and the teacher could not recall one of them; it is usually حَرْف وُجُودٍ لِوُجُودٍ).

    The teacher's reasoning: since Dr. Abdur Rahim put the other view in a footnote rather than in the main text, that shows which one he himself holds.

    4.3 Example — The Moon of Ibrāhīm

    فَلَمَّا رَأَى الْقَمَرَ بَازِغًا قَالَ هَٰذَا رَبِّي ۖ فَلَمَّا أَفَلَ قَالَ لَئِن لَّمْ يَهْدِنِي رَبِّي لَأَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْقَوْمِ الضَّالِّينَ (al-Anʿām 77)

    "When he saw the moon rising, he said, 'This is my Lord.' But when it set, he said, 'Unless my Lord guides me, I shall surely be among the people gone astray.'"

    The Whole Point Is the Jawāb

    If I say when I went there, you are waiting for what happened when I went there. The clause after لَمَّا just sets the stage: it gives you the time. The thing that happened at that time is the jawāb.

    Thinking It Through, or Demonstrating?

    Two sets of opinion on why Ibrāhīm عليه السلام said these things:

    1. He was genuinely investigating. He was born in a completely polytheist society: historians have found many relics in ancient Babylon pointing to worship of a sun god, moon goddesses and so many stars, and here is one youngster trying to reason that these can't all be his Lord.
    2. Because anbiyāʾ are born on the truth, he had already arrived at it, and this was a demonstration to startle his people into thinking and asking questions.

    4.4 The Forms of the Jawāb

    The book says the answer of لَمَّا can be a fiʿl māḍī, and with قَدْ + muḍāriʿ (sometimes, it might be) it adds that it might be a jumlah ismiyyah. But then it has to be مَقْرُونَة with something.

    مَقْرُون

    From the root of proximity, ق-ر-ن: something attached or made close. From the same root comes a word in the Quran about the angels (most likely أَوْ جَاءَ مَعَهُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ مُقْتَرِنِينَ, al-Zukhruf 53).

    (a) Attached to إِذَا الفُجَائِيَّة. This is the إِذَا of something sudden: I opened the door and lo and behold, there was a man there. The name comes from the word for all of a sudden.

    (The teacher said this word occurs in the Quran; the captions lost it. The Quran's word for suddenly is بَغْتَة, and the root ف-ج-أ does not itself occur.)

    فَإِذَا رَكِبُوا فِي الْفُلْكِ دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ فَلَمَّا نَجَّاهُمْ إِلَى الْبَرِّ إِذَا هُمْ يُشْرِكُونَ (al-ʿAnkabūt 65)

    "When they board a ship, they call upon Allah, sincere to Him in religion. But when He delivers them to land, at once they associate others with Him."

    بَرّ and بِرّ

    بَرّ is something that does not shake, as opposed to the sea, where your feet are not sure. That is why land is called بَرّ.

    A similar word from it is بِرّ, goodness. Why? Because it is stable. "Good is always stable, evil is always shaky." The ancient Arabs were extremely wise in their thinking.

    The Metaphorical Boat

    This image occurs quite a few times in the Quran. You don't have to be on a sea voyage. You could be in turmoil in your life, begging in front of Allah, and as soon as the problem is over you go back to your old ways.

    (b) Attached to فَ.

    وَإِذَا غَشِيَهُم مَّوْجٌ كَالظُّلَلِ دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ فَلَمَّا نَجَّاهُمْ إِلَى الْبَرِّ فَمِنْهُم مُّقْتَصِدٌ ۚ وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا كُلُّ خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ (Luqmān 32)

    "When waves cover them like canopies, they call upon Allah, sincere to Him in religion. But when He delivers them to land, among them are those who stop in between. And none denies Our signs except every perfidious ingrate."

    Again the waves can be the waves of darkness that every person meets in life. When the problem hits, their īmān is at its peak; back on firm ground, some are stuck between īmān and kufr.

    The students asked to have an āyah looked up whenever one came up, since the teacher was switching screens.

    Parsing فَمِنْهُم مُّقْتَصِدٌ

    • مِنْهُم cannot be the mubtadaʾ. It is a shibh jumlah and the khabar.
    • The mubtadaʾ, مُقْتَصِدٌ, is nakirah, and that is why the order reverses.
    • It carries فَ because it is the jawāb of لَمَّا and happens to be a jumlah ismiyyah.

    4.5 كَفُور and the Farmer

    كَفُور is a person who is very ungrateful. The word means to bury, and this is why a farmer is also a كَافِر: he buries the seed.

    كَمَثَلِ غَيْثٍ أَعْجَبَ الْكُفَّارَ نَبَاتُهُ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَكُونُ حُطَامًا (al-Ḥadīd 20)

    "Like a heavy rain whose produce delights the farmers; then it wilts, you see it turn yellow, then it becomes crushed debris."

    The āyah describes this worldly life as play, entertainment, embellishment, boasting, and competition in wealth and children. Here الْكُفَّار is not disbelievers: it means farmers.

    It is used for ingratitude because when you do not acknowledge a blessing, it is like you are hiding it somewhere and not even mentioning it.

    كَافِر is the ism fāʿil, and كَفُور is a صِيغَة المُبَالَغَة, one of its different intensive patterns. خَتَّار is translated here as perfidious: "whatever that means", the teacher said, asking for another translation.

    A Student's Note — Villages Called Kafr

    In Jordan, and probably Syria, there are villages called كَفْر + a name. The student had been confused why a village would be called disbeliever, but the original meaning, hiding the seed in the land, explains it: probably farmland. "That's interesting."

    See لَمَّا, إِذَا الفُجَائِيَّة, Root ب-ر-ر and Root ك-ف-ر.

    5. Where Is the Jawāb of فَلَمَّا?

    Now read Āyah 15 and try to work it out. When they went with him: what happened at that moment? A student's answer: وَأَوْحَيْنَا. "Okay, then why is there a wāw?" It is "a bit tricky, but it's very interesting."

    5.1 Two Schools

    Just as fiqh has its madhhabs, so does grammar. A madhhab here doesn't mean a religion; it means a school of thought.

    An Aside on Fiqh

    Besides the four well-known schools, there have been others whose fiqh is not preserved in its entirety. Sufyān al-Thawrī, for example, was a very big faqīh, by some accounts bigger than all four, but you only find bits and portions of his fiqh, not enough to follow him alone.

    In grammar, the two prominent schools are البَصْرَة and الكُوفَة.

    5.2 The Baṣrī View — The Jawāb Is Omitted

    The jawāb is مَحذُوف. The book reconstructs it as they did so: when they went with him and agreed to throw him in the well, they did throw him in. It is understood from the context, and indeed if you read the āyah, you find no mention of him actually being thrown in.

    5.3 The Kūfī View — An Extra Wāw

    The Kūfīs say the jawāb is in the text. So what is the wāw doing? They say it is not the wāw of ʿaṭf. They gave it a name: وَاو الاِقتِحَام, the wāw of intrusion.

    The class looked up the verb: to push or drag in, to introduce forcibly, to cram; to plunge or rush; to burst. According to them this wāw is added, and the jawāb can take it.

    5.4 The Example of al-Ṣāffāt

    فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ ۝ وَنَادَيْنَاهُ أَن يَا إِبْرَاهِيمُ (al-Ṣāffāt 103–104)

    "When they had both submitted and he had laid him down on his forehead, We called to him, 'O Ibrāhīm.'"

    (جَبِين is the forehead; تَلَّهُ was checked and means laid him down.) For the Kūfīs, وَنَادَيْنَاهُ is the jawāb, and it means simply نَادَيْنَاهُ.

    Homework

    How do the Baṣrīs explain this āyah? The teacher guesses that they say the jawāb is omitted here too (he was about to slaughter him, understood from the context), but does not know. Note it down, research it, and share it in the group.

    5.5 The Example of al-Zumar — The Open Gates

    وَسِيقَ الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا رَبَّهُمْ إِلَى الْجَنَّةِ زُمَرًا ۖ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءُوهَا وَفُتِحَتْ أَبْوَابُهَا وَقَالَ لَهُمْ خَزَنَتُهَا سَلَامٌ عَلَيْكُمْ طِبْتُمْ فَادْخُلُوهَا خَالِدِينَ (al-Zumar 73)

    "And those who feared their Lord will be driven to Paradise in groups until, when they reach it, while its gates stand open, its keepers will say to them, 'Peace be upon you. You have done well, so enter it, to abide eternally.'"

    زُمَرًا — In Groups

    Two interpretations: groups by status, so that the anbiyāʾ, the righteous and the Companions are each a different group from the laymen; or groups of people who loved each other, family and friends, going together, laughing and happy. "May Allah reunite us in Jannah."

    سَلَامٌ — Never with ال

    Whenever Allah mentions the greeting of peace in Jannah, it is indefinite, never with ال. The indefinite shows something very broad: the blessings and peace of Jannah are going to be many.

    The two readings:

    School The wāw of وَفُتِحَتْ Jawāb of حَتَّىٰ إِذَا Picture
    Kūfī Extra; the meaning is just فُتِحَتْ فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَابُهَا When they reach it, its doors are opened
    Baṣrī وَاو الحَال Omitted When they reach it, its doors already stand open

    The teacher personally prefers the wāw of ḥāl here, and asked the class not to think these are too many tangents: compare the people of the Fire two āyāt earlier:

    حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءُوهَا فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَابُهَا وَقَالَ لَهُمْ خَزَنَتُهَا أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ رُسُلٌ مِّنكُمْ (al-Zumar 71)

    (The captions have number 81; the āyah is 71.)

    Prison and Party

    Without the wāw, the doors were shut and are opened to admit this new group: the image of a jail, where the doors are kept shut, opened for a new prisoner, and closed again.

    With the wāw, its doors are already flung wide: the image of hospitality, like a big house throwing its doors open to welcome guests at a party.

    A Page of Sūrat al-Zumar

    The teacher memorized this sūrah for the sake of وَأَشْرَقَتِ الْأَرْضُ بِنُورِ رَبِّهَا (āyah 69), loved the āyah, and then developed such a bad block for this one page: "I still am not very good with this page."

    5.6 Student Tangents — Names for God

    A student noted that Aramaic, Hebrew and Arabic are all Semitic, from one proto-Semitic language, so the root for god is the same: Aramaic Aloho, Arabic Allāh, Hebrew Elohim, which is plural but takes singular verbs, and which they compared to a royal we. The teacher compared it to Allah calling Himself We, for majesty and royalty.

    The student added that the name of God is written with consonants only (Yahweh) and read aloud as Adonai. The teacher added that Jews avoid using the name and say Hashem, the Name, and that in both traditions taking the name of your Lord in vain is frowned upon.

    The Name اللَّه

    إِلَٰه is god in Arabic. There are two points of view: that اللَّه is a name not derived from anything, and that it is ال + إِلَٰه, which with frequent usage turned into اللَّه. On that view it is very interesting, because إِلَٰه has so many meanings, all very relevant to Allah. The teacher will look up the notes on this and share them on the group.

    5.7 Al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī

    If we take the Kūfī view, there are two candidates for the jawāb, and they are split:

    Mufassir Jawāb What happened at that moment
    al-Ṭabarī وَأَجْمَعُوا They agreed. Up until now they had been debating, but when they finally went with him, they settled on it.
    al-Qurṭubī وَأَوْحَيْنَا Allah sent this waḥy to Yūsuf عليه السلام.

    Both are giant names, so they carry equal weight. The Baṣrīs, as discussed, say the jawāb is omitted.

    See وَاو الاِقتِحَام, Basri and Kufi Schools and الحَال.

    6. لَتُنَبِّئَنَّهُم — Lām and Nūn

    Strip it down. Remove the lām, the pronoun هُم, and the emphatic nūn, and you are left with تُنَبِّئُ, your normal muḍāriʿ from نَبَّأَ / يُنَبِّئُ. The nūn is what changes its ending.

    When do you emphasise something with both lām and nūn? In the jawāb of an oath, as the class did in the workshop on oaths. So all the signs are here: there is an omitted, implied oath. Allah is taking an oath that you are surely going to inform them of this thing.

    6.1 The Three Conditions

    The book: when the muḍāriʿ happens to be (وَقَعَ, to happen, from which الوَاقِعَة, the great Happening) the jawāb of an oath, it is compulsory to emphasise it with lām and nūn, بِثَلَاثَةِ شُرُوطٍ (بِ here meaning upon; the meanings of بِ will come after the meanings of the lām).

    The Three Conditions

    1. It is مُستَقبَل: its meaning is for the future, not the present.
    2. It is مُثبَت: an affirmative sentence, with no negation.
    3. It is مُتَّصِل: the lām of the jawāb and the verb are connected, with nothing between them.

    لَتُنَبِّئَنَّهُم meets all three: connected directly to the lām, future in meaning (Yūsuf will inform his brothers), no negation.

    The book's example (reconstructed from the translation):

    وَاللَّهِ لَأَقُومَنَّ بِالدَّعْوَةِ إِلَى اللَّهِ بَعْدَ تَخَرُّجِي — By Allah, I will surely carry on calling to Allah after my graduation.

    قَامَ بِ means to stand firm on, to carry on with something. تَخَرُّج is graduation, when you leave your institute or madrasah.

    6.2 When a Condition Fails

    Negated: no emphasis, and even the lām does not appear. The whole topic was done in great detail in the workshop on oaths.

    وَاللَّهِ لَا أَنْسَى مُسَاعَدَتَكَ لَنَا أَبَدًا — By Allah, I will never forget your help to us. (reconstructed)

    Present meaning: the lām does enter, but no nūn.

    وَاللَّهِ لَأُحِبُّكَ — By Allah, I love you. (لَأُحِبَّنَّكَ would mean I will love you: I'm not loving you now.)

    By Allah, I detest lies. (the Arabic is lost; again no nūn)

    The Deciding Factor — Two Students Translate

    Sentence Meaning
    وَاللَّهِ لَأَسْكُنَنَّ المَدِينَةَ المُنَوَّرَةَ By Allah, I will live in Madīnah: plans for settling in the future
    وَاللَّهِ لَأَسْكُنُ المَدِينَةَ المُنَوَّرَةَ By Allah, I do live in Madīnah: you don't believe me, and I'm telling you where I live now

    One student rendered the second as I will live. There is no nūn, and that tells you it is present. The nūn is your deciding factor.

    Separated: no nūn. Whatever is in between, it doesn't matter what, as long as the verb is not directly after the lām.

    Between the lām and the verb Example
    A prepositional phrase, brought forward for emphasis By Allah, I am going to definitely complain about you… with the object of the verb fronted (the Arabic is lost). In the normal order the emphatic nūn could be used.
    Another prepositional phrase وَاللَّهِ لَبِمَكَّةَ أَسْكُنُ العَامَ القَادِمَ — By Allah, in Makkah I am going to live next year (reconstructed)
    سَوْفَ وَاللَّهِ لَسَوْفَ أُسَاعِدُ الفُقَرَاءَ — By Allah, I am going to help the poor (reconstructed)

    The book then cites a Quranic example of a separated verb (the sūrah and āyah are lost in the captions; وَلَسَوْفَ يُعْطِيكَ رَبُّكَ فَتَرْضَىٰ, al-Ḍuḥā 5, and لَإِلَى اللَّهِ تُحْشَرُونَ, Āl ʿImrān 158, are of this kind).

    6.3 Back to Our Āyah

    How Do We Know There Is an Oath?

    All of these examples mentioned the oath, but in Āyah 15 there is none. How do we know? Because of the lām and the nūn. They only gather on something when it is the jawāb of an oath. Whenever you see them together, there is an implied oath before them.

    See Jawāb al-Qasam and نُون التَّوكِيد.

    8. Key Lessons from This Session

    Summary of Lessons

    1. ʿAmal always means an effect on the iʿrāb. The lāms are classically only jāzimah and jārrah; the "nāṣibah" is a jārr lām with a hidden أَنْ.
    2. لَام التَّعلِيل works with nouns as well as verbs.
    3. The lām can mark the limit of a time or place: كُلٌّ يَجْرِي لِأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى.
    4. All the jārr lāms are one for grammar and very different in meaning.
    5. The lām can swear an oath, though rarely: لِلَّهِ here does not mean for Allah.
    6. رَبُّنَا يَعْلَمُ is in the place of an oath, as its إِنَّ + lām shows. Be very careful with Allah knows.
    7. جَعَلَ means to make into, to assume, to create, to begin. In the last meaning its khabar must be a muḍāriʿ sentence.
    8. Repeated Quranic stories are never the same: al-Qaṣaṣ tells Mūsā's story from the angle of resistance to tyranny, and it starts with a mother.
    9. لَمَّا of when is a ẓarf for the past, and it sets the stage for its jawāb.
    10. The jawāb of لَمَّا may be a māḍī, or a jumlah ismiyyah attached to إِذَا الفُجَائِيَّة or فَ.
    11. بَرّ and بِرّ share the idea of stability. Good is stable; evil is shaky.
    12. كُفَّار can mean farmers, those who bury the seed (al-Ḥadīd 20).
    13. In Āyah 15, the Baṣrīs say the jawāb is omitted; the Kūfīs find it in the text with an extra wāw, وَاو الاِقتِحَام.
    14. Among those taking that view, al-Ṭabarī takes وَأَجْمَعُوا and al-Qurṭubī takes وَأَوْحَيْنَا.
    15. The wāw of وَفُتِحَتْ (al-Zumar 73), read as wāw al-ḥāl, gives Jannah's gates already open, in contrast with the Fire's.
    16. Lām + nūn are compulsory in a muḍāriʿ jawāb that is future, affirmative and connected to the lām.
    17. Negated: no emphasis. Present: lām only. Separated: lām only. The nūn is the deciding factor between I will live and I do live.
    18. Lām and nūn together are themselves evidence of an implied oath.
